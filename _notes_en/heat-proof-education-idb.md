Extreme heat has become one of the main threats to access to, and the quality of, education in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC). The monograph *Educación a prueba de calor en América Latina y el Caribe* (Heat-proof education in Latin America and the Caribbean), from the Inter-American Development Bank's (IDB) Education Division, offers a comprehensive regional view of the problem: it synthesises the evidence on how heat affects learning, estimates how many schools, students and teachers are at risk, quantifies the cost of inaction and assesses the returns of different responses. Its central message is clear: we now know when heat begins to harm learning, where the most exposed schools are and which solutions work, yet extreme heat is still largely absent from education planning and budgets across the region.

## How extreme heat affects education

The report identifies impacts at three levels. First, heat reduces effective learning time. In 2024, heatwaves were the climate event that disrupted education most worldwide, affecting 171 million students. In the region, heat forced delays to the start of the school year in several Argentine provinces and in more than 2,300 schools in Rio Grande do Sul (Brazil) in 2025, and led 18 Mexican states to shorten the 2023 school year, affecting over 13 million students. Even when schools stay open, heat increases student and teacher absenteeism.

Second, heat undermines teaching and learning. Teachers' productivity on cognitive tasks can fall by up to 9% between 23 and 30 °C. Among students, high temperatures are associated with fatigue, dehydration, poorer attention and memory, and more disciplinary incidents. The key reference is a study using PISA data from 58 countries: each additional day above 26.7 °C is associated with a loss of roughly 1% of a school year's learning. Studies from Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica and Mexico confirm negative effects, which are larger in public and rural schools and among students from lower socio-economic backgrounds. The report also acknowledges effects on physical and mental health, including eco-anxiety, and on development from gestation through early childhood.

Third, heat has lifelong consequences. It can increase school dropout (in Brazil, more days above 34 °C raise dropout by 5.1%) and fuel a vicious circle between lower learning and interrupted school trajectories. According to the report's estimates, ten days of extreme heat per school year amount to 1.2 fewer years of learning and a reduction of around 11% in future earnings (between 8% and 19%, depending on the country).

## Which schools are most at risk

The analysis combines three dimensions: hazard (the frequency, duration and intensity of heatwaves during the school calendar), exposure (schools, students and teachers located in those areas) and vulnerability, approximated by the municipal poverty rate. It uses observed records for 2020–2025 and projections for 2026–2075 under the SSP3-7.0 scenario, a horizon consistent with the lifespan of school infrastructure. The main results are:

- Today, nearly 300,000 schools (36%), 27 million students and more than one million teachers (30%) are located in high or very high hazard areas, with 70 to 80 school days a year above the 26.7 °C threshold.
- Over the next 50 years, exposure would reach more than 357,000 schools (40%), 33.6 million students and 1.26 million teachers (35%).
- When low adaptive capacity is factored in, risk zones would grow from over 160,000 schools and 12 million students today to around 195,000 schools, more than 15 million students and some 590,000 teachers.
- Brazil, Mexico and Colombia account for 65% of the region's at-risk schools and 77% of its at-risk students, while Honduras (62%) and Belize (54%) have the largest share of their schools in risk zones.
- The cost of inaction, with just ten days of extreme heat a year, would exceed US$22 billion (PPP) annually in lost future earnings, equivalent to 0.18% of the GDP of the countries analysed.

The hazard is concentrated in Central America, the Caribbean, Colombia, Venezuela and northern Brazil, whereas the high Andes and the temperate Southern Cone show low levels. Patterns vary even within the same category, however: in northern Argentina heatwaves are short but very intense and coincide with December examinations, whereas in the Brazilian Amazon they last up to ten days.

## What works and what it returns

The report proposes two complementary lines of action. The first is resilient school infrastructure. Passive strategies (building orientation, cross-ventilation, eaves, reflective paint, insulation, shaded green schoolyards) can lower classroom temperatures by up to 9 °C at low cost and with no environmental impact. Active strategies, such as efficient air conditioning powered by solar photovoltaics, can achieve reductions of up to 20 °C, but with higher investment, running costs and environmental impact. The recommendation is to prioritise passive measures, add active ones where the climate requires it, and secure maintenance, water and electricity. The baseline is worrying: around 70% of audited classrooms in the region fell outside the thermal comfort range, and 15% of schools lack drinking water (30% in rural areas). Cost–benefit analyses for Brazil and El Salvador show that every measure assessed pays off, with returns of between 2 and over 40 times the investment; reflective paint offers the highest return.

The second line is flexible education services. Calendars can be adapted by shifting the start or end of the school year, redistributing holidays, adjusting school hours or adding reserve days to make up lost classes. Adding eight reserve days in Brazil, Colombia and Mexico would yield between 22 and 55 dollars per dollar invested. Distance learning is proposed as a last resort: telephone tutoring returns between 2.2 and 15.7 per dollar, and Uruguay's Ceibal programme shows the value of having capacity in place before an emergency strikes.

## Planning and financing resilience

The right response depends on the local heat pattern: a few hot days can be handled through calendar adjustments or tutoring, persistent heat requires resilient infrastructure, and heat concentrated in certain hours or months calls for changes to timetables or holidays. To guide these decisions, the report recommends integrating heat risk into education planning and linking education management information systems (EMIS), still at an early stage in most countries, with climate data and early warning systems.

On financing, more than 60% of education spending goes on teachers' salaries, leaving little room for adaptation. The report proposes mobilising additional resources (green education bonds such as those issued by Chile in 2020, international climate funds, including the education sector in National Adaptation Plans and NDCs, and public–private partnerships) and allocating them through heat-sensitive funding formulas, building on existing mechanisms such as Brazil's FUNDEB, Chile's Preferential School Subsidy (SEP) and São Paulo's PTRF.

## Scope of the analysis

The authors themselves present the regional results as a starting point to be complemented by national and subnational analyses. Vulnerability is approximated using municipal poverty owing to the lack of data on school infrastructure, exposure figures are minimum estimates given data gaps in some countries, and the loss estimates rely mainly on evidence generated outside the region.
